# Establish the brief and a visual direction

Start with what a visitor needs to understand and do. A portfolio may need to show the quality and relevance of the work before inviting an inquiry. A service site may need to establish location, availability and trust. A publication may need to make discovery and sustained reading effortless. These require different page hierarchies.

## Read the evidence

Inspect the existing homepage, an inner page, navigation and a representative content item. Note recurring visual decisions and whether they are deliberate or inconsistent. Use real copy to find the hierarchy: who this is for, what is offered, why to believe it, and what happens next. Retain useful brand recognition during a redesign. A requested content edit does not authorize a sitewide restyle.

Treat references as evidence of principles: the proportion of image to text, pace between sections, type contrast, grid rhythm or navigation density. Describe those principles in your own design. Do not duplicate another site's identity or assume every reference works for this audience.

## Make the direction concrete

Write a sentence connecting the subject to the visual choices. For example: “For an architecture studio, let large project photographs carry the page, use precise captions and an open grid, and reserve a restrained accent for navigation and inquiries.” This gives later decisions a reason.

Choose a few defining decisions rather than accumulating effects. Possible directions include:

| Direction | Useful decisions | What to watch |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Editorial | Strong headline/body contrast, captions, varied image scale, clear reading rhythm | Decorative type overwhelming long reading |
| Gallery | Generous image area, consistent project labeling, quiet controls | Visitors unable to find services or contact information |
| Practical service | Direct proposition, evidence near claims, visible next action, compact navigation | Repeating the same sales pitch in every section |
| Expressive brand | Distinctive type or color, deliberate asymmetry, a repeated graphic motif | Effects obscuring content or mobile interactions |

These are starting points, not mandatory templates. User preferences can call for dense, playful, traditional or deliberately understated design. Keep familiar controls usable even when the visual treatment is unusual.

## Write the page outline

Give each section one job and a meaningful heading. Sequence the visitor's questions; avoid adding sections solely to fill a familiar homepage pattern. Put the relevant evidence near the claim it supports. Use specific action labels such as “View residential projects” or “Request a consultation.” Never invent testimonials, client logos, credentials, project results or business facts to make a layout look finished.

For a new direction, define success before implementation: recognizable subject, obvious next step, legible real content and a coherent mobile version. Where assets or facts are missing, identify the gap and design around it honestly.
